Kita sa Bulsa, Kain sa Tyan

Gender inequality in access to food and control over household income.

Campaign goal

To reduce gender inequality in hunger by promoting fair access to food, shared household financial decisions, and women's economic empowerment, from the barangay to the world.

Who controls a married Filipino woman's cash earnings?

Currently married women aged 15 to 49, 2022 (Philippine Statistics Authority, 2023). Bar drawn to scale.

  • 55.5%Both spouses control it
  • 39.8%She controls it herself
  • 4.7%Her husband controls it

Control over income differs from home to home. So this campaign asks four questions: who earns, who decides how it is spent, who buys the food, and who goes without when money runs short.

The inequality

Hunger is not only about whether food exists

Gender inequality in hunger means people in the same household do not always have the same access to enough, nutritious food. Gender norms shape who earns income, who manages it, and who decides about food (FAO, 2025). Food insecurity should be examined by asking who has access to and control over the resources needed to get food, and not only who goes hungry.

What this does not mean. Men are not always less hungry than women. The point is that being the person who earns and handles money can give more immediate access to food.

Local, in the barangay

The one who holds the money

Picture a family where the father drives a tricycle or jeepney, or works in construction. During his shift he handles his own earnings and can buy food when he is hungry. His wife and children at home depend on the money or food he brings back after work.

Illustration developed by the group; control of earnings varies by household (PSA, 2023).

National, Philippines

Mothers as the buffer

Among urban poor households in Metro Manila, mothers often put the food needs of their children and other family members first, while also helping earn income. Earning money does not automatically mean equal food security. Women may adjust their own consumption to protect everyone else's.

Gregorio (2024)

Global

The gap appears in every region

Food insecurity is more common among adult women than men in every region of the world. A study of about 792,000 people in 137 countries (2014 to 2022) found women were more likely to be food insecure even after accounting for income, employment and education.

Research in South Asia also found that how food is shared among adults depends on income, social status and household circumstances, and the inequality shows most under severe shortage.

FAO (2025); Mane et al. (2025); Harris-Fry et al. (2017)

Solutions at three levels

From the barangay hall to the United Nations

Each level fixes a different part of the problem, and each depends on the others. Food aid alone does not change who decides. Law alone does not reach the kitchen table.

Local

Barangays, health centers, LGUs and community groups.

  • Livelihood and money skills for womenLGUs and Barangay Gender and Development (GAD) programs run skill-building, livelihood, financial literacy and money management sessions for stay-at-home mothers and women from low-income households.
  • Nutrition support that counts mothers tooBarangay health centers strengthen food assistance, supplementary feeding and nutrition education for mothers and children, recognizing that some mothers eat less so their children can eat.
  • Community feeding that reaches the right homesBarangays prioritize households with pregnant women, single mothers, female-headed households and limited income, working with health centers, nutrition scholars and community groups.

National

Congress, agencies and LGUs under Philippine law.

  • Enforce the Magna Carta of WomenStrengthen implementation of Republic Act No. 9710, especially Section 20 on food security and productive resources, which covers access to safe, nutritious food, training and economic opportunities for marginalized women, pregnant and lactating mothers and their children.
  • Gender-responsive food assistanceEncourage LGUs to identify food-insecure households and plan for the specific needs of women, including those who skip meals for their families.
  • Family resource workshopsCommunity workshops on fair food distribution, budgeting and shared financial decisions.
  • Measure who is reachedAgencies and LGUs collect sex-disaggregated data on food insecurity and check whether assistance reaches vulnerable people, as the Magna Carta's gender-responsive planning and monitoring provisions call for.

Global

UN Sustainable Development Goals and international partners.

  • SDG 2: Zero HungerTarget 2.1 aims to end hunger and give everyone, especially people in poverty and vulnerable situations, access to safe, nutritious and sufficient food all year.
  • SDG 5: Gender EqualityTarget 5.4 recognizes unpaid care and domestic work. Target 5.a promotes women's equal rights to economic resources and to ownership and control over property and financial resources.
  • Put the goals into barangay practiceQuarterly assessments of food-insecure households, livelihood training and shared household decision-making, and evaluation every six months that tracks households assisted, changes in food insecurity, skipped meals and how resources are allocated.
  • What international partners addBodies such as the FAO and UN agencies can support comparable data, shared lessons and funding so that local programs are judged against the same goals everywhere.

How the three levels work together

Local gathers the facts

Quarterly barangay assessments show which homes lack food and who skips meals.

National turns facts into policy

Sex-disaggregated data tells agencies whether the Magna Carta's food and resource provisions are working.

Global sets the standard

SDG 2 and SDG 5 give shared targets, comparison and support for the national effort.

Support flows back down

Stronger policy and shared goals back barangay programs with resources and legitimacy.

Evidence from real programs

Cash helps. Shared decisions finish the job.

Two large cash transfer programs, one in the Philippines and one in Brazil, show what helps and what is still missing.

Philippines

Pantawid Pamilya (4Ps)

The government's flagship program gives cash to poor households on condition that they invest in their children's health and schooling. The third impact evaluation found positive effects on household welfare, including income and food security.

A study of 4Ps households in Calauan and San Pablo City, Laguna, found that transfers involving women had modest effects on their time, while household gender relations largely stayed the same.

Orbeta et al. (2021); Alinsunurin (2018)

Brazil

Bolsa Família

Payments go preferentially to the female head of household. Researchers at IFPRI asked whether women's control of the money gives them more say in decisions. They found it could be a pathway to empowerment, particularly for women in urban areas, but no significant increase in decision-making power for rural women.

de Brauw et al. (2014); IFPRI summary

The lesson for this campaign

Pair money with voice

Income support raises food security, but it does not by itself change who decides or who eats less. That is why the campaign pairs food assistance with financial literacy, shared budgeting and gender-responsive monitoring, and adapts them to each community.

Implementation plan

A twelve-month plan that starts in one barangay

Months 1 to 3

Listen and raise awareness

  • School and barangay talks using this campaign's facts
  • Social media posts and short videos under #KitaSaBulsaKainSaTyan
  • A simple baseline survey: who earns, who decides, who buys food, who skips meals
  • Partner meetings with the barangay GAD focal person and health center
Months 4 to 9

Run the pilot

  • Financial literacy and livelihood workshops for mothers and women from low-income homes
  • Feeding and nutrition education prioritizing the most food-insecure households
  • Quarterly assessments of food-insecure households to direct assistance
  • Family resource management sessions on fair food sharing and budgeting
Month 10 onward

Evaluate and expand

  • Review every six months: households assisted, change in food insecurity, skipped meals, use of resources
  • Report sex-disaggregated results to the barangay and the LGU
  • Adjust programs, then share the model with neighboring barangays

Sustainability and innovation

Built to keep going after the campaign ends

Why it lasts

  • It uses structures that already exist: barangay GAD programs, health centers and the Magna Carta of Women.
  • Local women and community members are trained to lead the workshops, so the skills stay in the barangay.
  • Every six months the numbers are reviewed, so weak parts are fixed instead of repeated.
  • Partnerships across households, schools, LGUs, NGOs and agencies share the work and the cost.

What is new about it

  • Counts sacrifice, not just supply. Evaluations track skipped meals alongside food availability.
  • Looks inside the household. It treats control of income and unpaid work as part of hunger.
  • Youth-made media. Short videos and infographics in Filipino and English.
  • A tool for the kitchen table. The Fair Plate, Fair Pay checklist, next to this card.
